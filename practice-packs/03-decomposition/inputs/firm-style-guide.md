# Firm style guide — pre-suit demand letters

> Apply to all pre-suit demand correspondence. These rules exist because settlement leverage erodes when the demand letter sounds either timid or hysterical. Be exact.

## Voice

- Third-person formal. **Never use "I" or "we"**; refer to the firm as "this firm" and to the client by name.
- Active voice. No "it is regrettable that…", no "one might consider…". Use "Acme is entitled to" rather than "Acme should be entitled to".
- No threats of personal consequence ("you will be personally liable…"). Threaten only contractual and statutory remedies.

## Structure (exact section order)

1. **Recital of facts.** Numbered. One fact per paragraph. No legal characterization in this section.
2. **Legal theory.** Tied to specific Agreement sections by number and to controlling Delaware law.
3. **The demand.** A precise dollar figure, a precise release of obligations, and any non-monetary terms. Use a numbered list.
4. **Deadline and consequence.** Specific date, fourteen (14) calendar days from the letter's date, and a clear statement of what happens at the deadline.

## Banned phrases

- "It has come to our attention" — use "Acme advises this firm" or state the fact directly.
- "Without prejudice" in a demand body (use it only in the heading or footer if at all; misuse waives protection).
- "Time is of the essence" without a specific date that follows.
- "Reasonable" used as the only qualifier for a dollar figure. State the figure.
- Hedges: "approximately", "in the region of", "around" when followed by a settlement number. Be exact or omit.

## Numeric discipline

- Every dollar figure must tie to a fact paragraph by number. (For example: "the $153,000 referenced in Paragraph 7 above".)
- Spell out figures over $1,000 in both words and numerals on first use: "one hundred fifty-three thousand dollars ($153,000)".
- Calendar dates in long form: "March 4, 2024", never "3/4/24".

## What never goes in a demand letter

- Settlement floor. Never state the lowest figure the client would accept. Only state the demand.
- Litigation preference. Never write "the client would prefer to settle". Only state the consequence of non-payment.
- Internal-only damages estimates that have not been documented (for example, the rough "$80,000-$120,000" operational losses in the brief — those are not litigation-ready and must not appear in the letter).
